# When do property insurers actually leave a market?

**California wildfire as the worked precedent — and what it implies for Miami / coastal Florida**

Compiled 2026-08-20 for the insurance-fragility layer of the Miami flood-risk project.
Companion data file: `insurer_exit_timeline.csv` (59 dated, sourced actions, 2018-11-08 → 2026-07-31, CA/FL/LA/CO/TX/MA/US).

**Source convention used throughout.** `[P]` = primary source (state DOI bulletin or filing, FAIR Plan / Citizens / TWIA publication, company press release or shareholder letter, GAO / CRS / legislative auditor, peer-reviewed). `[S]` = secondary reporting only — treat the number as indicative, not citable-as-fact. `[?]` = could not verify; explicitly flagged.

## 0. Bottom line up front

The project thesis — *insurer behaviour is a leading indicator of climate repricing, and the trigger is near-term loss experience plus rate-approval friction, not long-run climate projections* — **survives the evidence, with two amendments.**

**What the record supports.** In every documented US market exit 2018-2026, the proximate cause is one of three things: (1) a loss-ratio shock from a discrete catastrophe year, (2) reinsurance cost or unavailability, or (3) inability to get a rate approved. Long-run climate projections appear in *rhetoric* — Commissioner Lara's 2023 statement blamed "climate change challenges" [P] — but never in a company's operative decision document. State Farm General's own filing rationale is an accounting statement about the past nine years, not a forecast [P].

**Amendment 1: litigation is a fourth first-order driver, and in Florida it dominated.** Florida's 2020-2023 collapse was not primarily a catastrophe-loss event. In 2020 Florida generated ~9% of US homeowners claims and ~79% of US homeowners claims litigation [P]. Ten-plus insolvencies followed. When the legislature removed one-way attorney fees and post-loss AOB (SB 2-A, signed 2022-12-16) [S-law-firm summary, statute is primary], the market reversed within two years *with hurricane exposure unchanged* — Hurricane Milton ($30-50B insured, October 2024) [P] did not stop the recovery. That is close to a natural experiment, and it argues that "climate risk" was not the binding constraint in Florida at all.

**Amendment 2: the fast/slow distinction is the load-bearing insight, and it is sharper than "loss experience beats projections."** Insurers write one-year contracts. Any hazard whose expected loss changes by low single-digit percent per year is *repriceable* and does not trigger exit. Exit happens when a single year's realised loss exceeds several years of premium at once. Sea level rise is the former; storm surge is the latter. This is why the null result below is not an anomaly — it is the prediction.

**The null result (answered in full in §3.5): there is no documented case, anywhere in the United States, of a property insurer withdrawing from a market and citing sea level rise projections as the reason.** Not one. The closest artefacts are (a) a New Zealand modelling study projecting such retreat in the 2040s [P], and (b) the Massachusetts FAIR Plan turning into a de facto coastal plan while carriers cite storms, wind and downpours — never SLR [S].

### 1.3 The regulatory mechanism — Proposition 103 and what changed in December 2024

**The pre-2024 regime.** Proposition 103 (1988) requires prior approval of homeowners rates and, as implemented in California Code of Regulations Title 10 §§2641.1-2644.28, produced two constraints found in no other state:

1. **No forward-looking catastrophe models.** Rates had to be built on historical loss experience — in practice a rolling 20-year average of wildfire losses. CBO states it directly: California "prohibits insurers from using wildfire catastrophe models that simulate future events in the rate-setting process" ([CBO, *Climate Change, Disaster Risk, and Homeowner's Insurance*, August 2024](https://www.cbo.gov/system/files/2024-08/59918-Climate-Change-Insurance.pdf)) **[P]**.
2. **No pass-through of net reinsurance cost.** California was the only state that excluded net reinsurance cost from the ratemaking expense build-up entirely [P-CDI/IJ].

The combination is the mechanism the thesis needs: a carrier facing a genuine step-change in expected loss could neither *model* it nor *finance* it through the rate. That is why "reinsurance costs" appears in every single stated exit rationale in California and why Allstate's spokesperson added "unable to quickly adjust prices due to state regulations."

**The Sustainable Insurance Strategy — dated sequence** (all from [CDI's Sustainable Insurance Strategy page](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/180-climate-change/sustainable-insurance-strategy.Cfm) **[P]** unless noted):

| Date | Action |
|---|---|
| 2024-07-26 | FAIR Plan modernization announced |
| 2024-08-09 | Rate-review reforms moved to implementation |
| 2024-11-14 | Step to increase availability in wildfire-distressed areas |
| 2024-11-21 | Public input invited on coverage expansion |
| **2024-12-13** | **Catastrophe modeling regulation** filed with the Secretary of State by OAL. Forward-looking wildfire cat models permitted in ratemaking for the first time; models must credit mitigation/hardening. In exchange, major insurers must write comprehensive policies in wildfire-distressed areas at **no less than 85% of their statewide market share**. PRID petitions accepted from **2025-01-02**. ([CDI release 062-2024](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/0400-news/0100-press-releases/2024/release062-2024.cfm)) **[P]** |
| **2024-12-30** | **Net Cost of Reinsurance in Ratemaking regulation** issued. Reinsurance cost becomes a permitted expense, treated like claims handling or commissions — but subject to an industry **benchmark cap**: an insurer that spends above the standard cannot pass the excess to policyholders. ([Insurance Journal, 2024-12-30](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/west/2024/12/30/806461.htm)) [S reporting a P regulation] |
| 2025-03-28 | FAIR Plan commercial limits raised to **$20M/building, $100M/location**; eligibility extended to HOAs, affordable-housing developers, farmers, builders ([CDI release 028-2025](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/0400-news/0100-press-releases/2025/release028-2025.cfm)) **[P]** |
| by Oct 2025 | **Three forward-looking wildfire catastrophe models reviewed and approved** ([CDI SIS summary, 2025-10-14](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/180-climate-change/upload/Sustainable-Insurance-Strategy-CDI-Summary-10-14-25.pdf)) **[P]** |

**The timing is the finding.** Both regulations landed in the last three weeks of December 2024. The Palisades and Eaton fires started **2025-01-07** — roughly three weeks later. California fixed the rate mechanism immediately before the loss event that tested it. Farmers' December 2024 reversal is the cleanest causal evidence available anywhere in this file that **rate-approval friction, not hazard, was the binding constraint**: Farmers expanded writing by 36% (7,000 → 9,500/month) with *zero* change in California's wildfire hazard, citing the regulations.

### 1.5 What actually triggered the exits — the quantitative record

This is the strongest evidence in the file, and it is all backward-looking accounting:

**State Farm General** ([State Farm, *State Farm and the California Insurance Marketplace*](https://newsroom.statefarm.com/state-farm-in-california-understanding-the-issues/)) **[P]**:
- Over a nine-year period, **"for every $1.00 collected in premium, SFG paid $1.26"** — a ~126% pure loss ratio before expenses.
- **Over $5 billion in cumulative underwriting losses** across that period.
- **Policyholder surplus: ~$4.16B (2016) → ~$1.04B (end-2024)** — a ~75% erosion, including a >$300M decline during 2024 alone.
- January 2025 wildfires: **~$7.6B gross direct loss**, of which only **~$212M retained after reinsurance** — the reinsurance program worked; the balance-sheet problem was the nine years that preceded it.
- **>$165M** FAIR Plan assessment share; **$400M** parent surplus note received June 2025.
- Claims paid: >$4.2B by 2025-07-06; >$5B on 13,500 claims by 2025-11-12; >$5.7B by 2026-05-04.

**Market-wide.** Milliman puts the California homeowners **rolling 10-year underwriting result (2013-2022) at −10.9% of direct earned premium**, on a market of **$13.7B direct written premium (2023)** ([Milliman, 2025-01-22](https://www.milliman.com/en/insight/california-homeowners-insurance-los-angeles-wildfires)) **[P-actuarial]**. A decade of ~−11% underwriting margin, with no ability to model forward or recover reinsurance cost, is a sufficient explanation without invoking any climate projection.

**Reinsurance cost.** The Guy Carpenter **US property catastrophe rate-on-line index** rose roughly **66% cumulatively from the 2017 soft-market low to the 2024 peak**, then reversed: **−6.2% at 1 Jan 2025, −12% at 1 Jan 2026, −14% at 1 Apr 2026, ~−16% year-to-date by mid-2026 — ~22% below the 2024 peak** ([Artemis, Guy Carpenter US property cat ROL index](https://www.artemis.bm/us-property-cat-rate-on-line-index/)) [S publishing P index]. The exits cluster in **2022-2024** — exactly the window when reinsurance was repricing hardest. `[?]` Exact index point values by year were not obtainable; only the percentage changes above are documented.

**Non-renewal rates and risk targeting.** JCHS finds Altadena (91001) at **1.7%** and Pacific Palisades (90272) at **1.8%** insurer non-renewal in 2022, versus **1.3% California** and **1.2% national** — and that "nonrenewal rates are significantly higher in ZIP codes with relatively high expected losses from climate-related perils" ([JCHS, 2025-05-27](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/blog/californias-homeowners-insurance-market-national-bellwether)) **[P-academic]**. Two contextual caveats: (i) CDI notes **75-80% of all non-renewals are policyholder-initiated**; only **20-25% are insurer-initiated** [P]; (ii) even in the highest-risk ZIPs the insurer-initiated rate is under 2% — carriers shed books slowly, by attrition, which is exactly what a leading indicator looks like.

**The event itself.** Palisades + Eaton: Verisk industry insured loss **$28-35B** ([Verisk, 2025-01-22](https://www.verisk.com/company/newsroom/verisk-estimates-industry-insured-losses-for-the-palisades-and-eaton-fires-will-fall-between-usd-28-billion-and-usd-35-billion/)) **[P]**; Chubb's Evan Greenberg put the industry at **$45-50B** with **$1.5B pre-tax to Chubb** [P]. CDI's claims tracker reports **38,835 residential property claims** and **~$22.0B paid** as of its March reporting ([CDI claims information](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/180-climate-change/upload/Auto-Residential-and-Commercial-Property-Claims-Information-as-of-March-3rd-Palisades-and-Eaton-Fires.pdf)) **[P]** — `[?]` the automated extraction of the per-line breakdown from this PDF was internally inconsistent; the headline claim count and paid total are reported here, but re-read the PDF manually before quoting the line-of-business split.

**And the industry's own account of causation.** Greenberg, [Chubb 2024 letter to shareholders](https://about.chubb.com/stories/2024-shareholder-letter.html) **[P]**:

> "state regulators deny insurers the ability to charge an adequate price and restrict our flexibility to tailor coverage, they drive away insurance availability and foolishly suppress economic price signals"

> "The state suppressed the industry's ability to charge a fair price for wildfire-exposed coverage."

> "For Chubb, we want a 15% return on capital. Without an adequate return, the private sector can't attract the capital necessary to cover growing exposures."

Note what is absent: any reference to a climate projection, a warming scenario, or a forward hazard horizon beyond the next policy period. The complaint is entirely about **price adequacy today**.

## 2. Florida — the parallel and where it diverges

### 2.1 The 2020-2023 crisis: litigation, not catastrophe

**The defining statistic.** In 2020 Florida homeowners filed roughly **9% of all US homeowners claims** but accounted for approximately **79% of all US homeowners claims litigation** ([Insurance Journal / Claims Journal reporting Florida OIR + NAIC data, 2026-07-22](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/southeast/2026/07/22/878610.htm)) [S reporting P data]. FLOIR's own May 2024 market update gives the 2022 cut: **14.9% of the nation's claims, 70.8% of the nation's litigation** ([FLOIR, May 2024](https://floir.gov/docs-sf/property-casualty-libraries/property-insurance-market-overview/insurance-update-may-2024.pdf?sfvrsn=1f0259bb_2)) **[P]**.

**Insolvencies.** Eleven-plus Florida property insurers were declared insolvent 2020-2023: Windhaven (2020); Gulfstream P&C, American Capital Assurance (2021); St. Johns, Avatar, Lighthouse, Southern Fidelity, Weston, FedNat (2022); United Property & Casualty (2023) ([FIGA](https://figafacts.com/category/insolvency/); [FLOIR company actions](https://floir.gov/resources-and-reports/recent-company-actions)) **[P]**. Florida homeowners insurers accounted for the **majority of all US insurer liquidations in 2022** [S-S&P Global].

**Withdrawals by still-solvent carriers.** Farmers exited its Florida-branded book in July 2023 (~100,000 policies, ~30% of its FL book; Foremost and Bristol West continued) [S]; AAA non-renewed a "small percentage" of higher-exposure FL policies (July 2023) [S]; Progressive reduced its FL property book; Bankers Insurance and AIG's Lexington exited [S].

**Reinsurance was a co-driver but not the primary one.** FLOIR reports Florida risk-adjusted reinsurance costs rose an average of **27% from 2022 to 2023** — notably *less* than the 50-60% the market had predicted **[P]**.

### 2.2 The reforms and the reversal

- **2022-05-26** — SB 2-D (special session): reinsurance assistance, roof deductible options, attorney-fee limits.
- **2022-12-16** — **SB 2-A signed**. Eliminates one-way attorney's fees for residential property suits; **prohibits post-loss assignment of benefits** for policies issued after 2023-01-01; requires an adverse court adjudication before a bad-faith claim; cuts claim-filing deadlines from 2 years to 1 (new) and 3 years to 18 months (supplemental); prompt-pay changes effective 2023-03-01; **phases in a flood-coverage requirement for Citizens policyholders**. ([Clyde & Co summary](https://www.clydeco.com/en/insights/2023/03/historic-florida-insurance-reforms-under-sb-2-a)) [S summarising P statute]

**The result, from FLOIR's own [Property Insurance Stability Report, 1 July 2026](https://floir.gov/docs-sf/default-source/property-and-casualty/stability-unit-reports/july-2026-isu-report.pdf) [P]:**

| Metric | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| FL share of US homeowners suits | **79%** (2020) → 73.15% (2024) | **41.29%** (2025) |
| FL share of US homeowners claims | ~9% (2020) → 12.33% (2024) | **4.85%** (2025) |
| Legal service-of-process filings | — | **−23%** (2023→24), **−25%** (2024→25), **−25%** (first 5 months of 2026 YoY) |
| Defence cost per claim | $947.38 (2022) | **$720.00** (2025), −24% |
| Domestic insurer net income | Losses >$1B/yr for three consecutive years pre-2023 | **Positive in 2024**, continued into 2025 |
| Pooled combined ratio | — | **83% in 2025** — lowest in more than a decade |
| New residential carriers | — | **21 new companies approved** since the reforms, plus 1 recapitalised out of runoff and 1 acquired |
| Rate filings | Years of increases | **44 companies filed decreases** for 2024+; **48 filed 0%** |
| 2026 reinsurance | +27% risk-adjusted (2022→23) | **~half of ARDC insurers seeing 15-25% risk-adjusted reductions**; ~10% average minimum decrease |

**The natural experiment.** Hurricane Milton made landfall **2024-10-09** with industry insured losses estimated at **$30-50B** ([Verisk](https://s29.q4cdn.com/767340216/files/doc_news/Verisk-Estimates-Industry-Insured-Losses-for-Hurricane-Milton-Will-Range-Between-USD-30-Billion-to-USD-50-Billion-2024.pdf)) **[P]** — comparable to Palisades/Eaton. Florida's market absorbed it and *kept recovering*: positive 2024 net income, an 83% combined ratio in 2025, 21 new entrants. California's market did not. The difference is not hazard. It is that Florida fixed its cost driver (litigation) and its rate mechanism (Florida uses a file-and-use system without Prop 103's modelling and reinsurance prohibitions), while California only fixed its rate mechanism three weeks before its shock event.

### 2.4 Why wind can recover while flood cost rises — the mechanism

This is not a paradox. Wind and flood in Florida are **different perils, sold by different entities, priced by different mechanisms, under different law.**

| | **Wind / homeowners** | **Flood** |
|---|---|---|
| Who carries the risk | Private admitted carriers + Citizens (state residual) | **NFIP — a federal programme** (~96%+ of FL residential flood policies); small private/E&S segment |
| Rate mechanism | Florida file-and-use, FLOIR review; carriers can and do file **decreases** | FEMA sets full-risk rates actuarially; **no state regulator, no competitive pressure** |
| Rate constraint | Market competition + regulator | **Statutory 18%/yr cap, 42 U.S.C. §4015(e)** — a legislated glide path |
| What drove 2020-23 cost | **Litigation/AOB** — a legal-system cost, removable by statute | **Underpricing legacy** — subsidised rates being retired |
| Direction of travel | **Down** since 2024 (litigation reform worked) | **Up**, mechanically, every year until full-risk rates are reached |
| What "exit" looks like | Carrier insolvency, non-renewal, withdrawal | **Consumer lapse and non-renewal** — there is no carrier to withdraw |

The two can move in opposite directions because **the driver of the wind crisis was legal, not physical, and was fixed by legislation; whereas the driver of flood cost pressure is a statutory repricing schedule that runs independently of any storm, reform or market condition.**

**The flood glide path, quantified** ([GAO-23-105977, July 2023](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-23-105977.pdf)) **[P]**:
- As of 2022-12-31, **~66% of renewed NFIP policies were still below their full-risk premium**.
- **Median current premium $689 vs median full-risk premium $1,288** — an ~**90%** gap still to be closed.
- ~**95% of policies reach full-risk rates by 2037**; essentially all by **2049**.
- ~**9% of policies will require increases exceeding 300%**.
- Florida is among five Gulf Coast states with the highest increases; that region holds **61% of NFIP policies**.

**Florida specifically** (FEMA's projected RR2.0 analysis, republished via ASFPM ArcGIS services — see this project's `NOTES_rr2.md`): of **1,727,811** Florida NFIP policies, **80.2% see increases** and 19.8% decreases; **4.22% face increases above $20/month**. **Vintage caveat:** these are FEMA's *pre-implementation projections* comparing May 2020 legacy premiums to modelled full-risk rates, last modified October 2021. They give direction and distribution, not current premium levels.

**Note on forward-looking risk in RR2.0.** RR2.0 rating variables are "flood types, distance from a flooding source, frequency of flood, elevation, and the cost to rebuild a property" ([FEMA RR2.0 FAQs](https://agents.floodsmart.gov/sites/default/files/media/document/2025-07/fema-nfip-risk-rating-2.0-FAQs.pdf)) **[P]**. `[?]` **I could not find an authoritative FEMA statement confirming or denying that RR2.0 incorporates *future* sea level rise projections as opposed to current-condition flood frequency.** Secondary reporting ([NBC Miami, 2022-04-01](https://www.nbcmiami.com/investigations/fema-changes-to-flood-insurance-renewals-now-factor-in-sea-level-rise/2727141/)) says RR2.0 "factors in ... sea-level rise" but does not establish whether that is present-day or projected. congress.gov's CRS product IN11777 was blocked by robots.txt. **Treat this as an open question, not a settled fact.**

## 3. Other precedents

### 3.1 Louisiana — the purest catastrophe-driven collapse

The [Louisiana Legislative Auditor's *Financial Condition of the Residential Property Insurance Market*, 2022-10-13](https://app.lla.state.la.us/publicreports.nsf/0/9d5c25a709f476a3862588da005c6930/$file/000283c0.pdf) **[P]** is the cleanest primary document in this entire file:

- **11 property insurers became insolvent between July 2021 and September 2022**: Access Home, Americas, FedNat, Gulfstream P&C, Gulfstream Select, Lighthouse Excalibur, Lighthouse Property, Maison, State National Fire, Southern Fidelity, Weston.
- **Six of the eleven failed directly because of inadequate Hurricane Ida reinsurance.** The other five involved Florida-book contagion.
- Stated causes, verbatim: *"impacts of the calendar year 2020 and 2021 hurricanes, the perceived risk or exposure to potential losses in Louisiana, and/or the inability to obtain reinsurance."*
- **Louisiana Citizens: 35,670 policies / $6.7B TIV (Jan 2021) → 112,035 / $33.3B (Aug 2022)** — **+214% policies, +397% exposure in 19 months.**
- Underlying losses: Laura **$7.4B** / 177,691 claims; Delta $751M; Zeta $423M; **Ida $9.8B** / 460,709 claims. **2020-21 total: $18.4B across 784,436 claims.**

**Recovery, and how it was bought.** Louisiana ran the **Insure Louisiana Incentive Program** (capital grants to carriers writing Citizens takeouts, 2023) and the **Louisiana Fortify Homes Program** (mitigation grants, launched 2023-10-02) ([LDI](https://ldi.la.gov/news/press-releases/9-13-23-louisiana-fortify-homes-program-launches-october-2-for-eligible-homeowners-insured-by-louisiana-citizens)) **[P]**. Citizens depopulation followed: Round 19 (assumption 2024-04-01) transferred **8,415 policies / ~$2.4B**; Round 20 (2024-12-01) transferred **13,481 / ~$4.49B** ([LA Citizens FY2024 financial report](https://app.lla.state.la.us/PublicReports.nsf/0/AE4B67E34358EBE086258CC900554AAA/$FILE/00008151.pdf)) **[P]**.

**Diagnosis: mechanism (a) — a catastrophe loss shock — with (b) reinsurance as the transmission channel.** No regulatory friction story, no litigation story, no climate-projection story.

### 3.2 Colorado — wildfire, and a state building a residual market from zero

- **2021-12-30** — Marshall Fire, Boulder County: costliest wildfire in Colorado history.
- **2023-05-12** — **HB23-1288 signed** (effective 2023-08-07), creating the **Colorado FAIR Plan Association** — the state's first property insurer of last resort ([Colorado General Assembly](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/HB23-1288)) **[P]**.
- **2025-2026** — the Colorado FAIR Plan is in "Phase 1," building products and systems ([Colorado FAIR Plan](https://www.coloradofairplan.com/about)) **[P]**.

`[?]` **Colorado FAIR Plan policy counts are not published** anywhere I could locate — neither the FAIR Plan site, the Colorado DOI toolkit, nor DORA. Neither is a Colorado-specific post-Marshall non-renewal series. If Colorado matters to the project, this data has to be requested from the Colorado DOI directly.

**Diagnosis: mechanism (a), with a two-year institutional lag.** Colorado is interesting precisely because it had *no* residual market — the FAIR Plan is the state's response to a loss shock, created 17 months after the fire and writing business ~3 years after.

### 3.3 Texas — a large, stable coastal residual market

TWIA, the statutory coastal wind residual market, as of **Q1 2026**: **286,251 policies in force, $127.1B exposure, average residential premium $2,877**, and **53% of the residential market share within its coastal coverage area** as of 2024 ([TWIA Media Kit, May 2026](https://www.twia.org/wp-content/uploads/2026.05-TWIA-Media-Kit.pdf)) **[P]**.

The Texas signal is different in kind: it is not a crisis but a **standing equilibrium in which the private market declines to write roughly half of coastal wind risk and has done so for decades.** That is what "insurers have left" looks like *after* it has stabilised — no headlines, no exits, just a permanent public backstop. It is the most likely long-run steady state for coastal Florida wind, and worth watching as the counterfactual.

### 3.4 Massachusetts — the closest thing to a coastal-driven residual market shift

The Massachusetts FAIR Plan grew from **158,660 policies (FY2023) to 173,000+ (FY2024)**, a ~9% increase and the **first increase since 2017**. On Cape Cod and the islands, the share of homes insured through the FAIR Plan went from **33% (2023) to 40% (2024)**; coastal Bristol and Plymouth counties plus the Cape and islands now account for **55% of all FAIR Plan enrollees, up from 51% in 2020**. One expert described the plan shifting "from primarily an urban plan to now a coastal plan." ([CommonWealth Beacon](https://commonwealthbeacon.org/environment/mass-home-insurer-of-last-resort-sees-spike-in-enrollment/)) [S]

**Critically: the article documents 14 of the top 25 insurers implementing measures to reduce climate exposure — and the perils named are "heavy downpours," "extreme weather events," "floods," "high winds," "severe storms." Sea level rise is not cited by any carrier.** Massachusetts is the strongest available test case for a coastal-driven withdrawal, and it still comes back storm-driven.

### 3.5 **Is there ANY documented case of a carrier withdrawing explicitly because of sea level rise projections?**

**No. This is a genuine null result, and I searched for it from six independent angles.**

| Search angle | Result |
|---|---|
| Direct: carrier withdrawal citing SLR | Nothing. Returns generic "coastal insurance crisis" commentary. |
| NAIC Climate Risk Disclosure Survey / Ceres scorecards | No documented case of an underwriting withdrawal attributed to SLR. |
| Senate Budget Committee non-renewal investigation (2024-12-18): data from ~24 insurers, ~65% of the national homeowners market, **249 million policy records, 2018-2023** [P] | Identifies high non-renewal rates in FL, CA, LA, coastal NJ, coastal NC, southern New England. **The dataset is non-renewal counts. It contains no insurer statements of rationale, and the report does not claim any carrier cited SLR.** |
| Environmental Law Institute, *The Coastal Property Insurance Crisis* read-ahead (2024-03-13) [P-NGO] | Explicitly checked. **No insurer identified as citing SLR projections.** The named carriers (State Farm, Allstate, Farmers, AIG) are listed without rationale. Its framing is that insurers anticipate "sharp increases in damage claims" — i.e. loss experience. |
| CBO, *Climate Change, Disaster Risk, and Homeowner's Insurance* (Aug 2024) [P] | Discusses SLR at length as a **property-value and federal-exposure** problem. Attributes insurer withdrawal to price regulation, loss quantification difficulty, capital adequacy and **recent catastrophic events**. No SLR-driven withdrawal identified. |
| Coastal-state-specific (MA/RI, NC, NJ) | Carriers cite storms, wind, downpours. Never SLR. |

**The single closest artefact — and why it proves the point rather than refuting it.** Storey, Owen, Zammit & Noy, ["Insurance retreat in residential properties from future sea level rise in Aotearoa New Zealand," *Climatic Change* 177:44, 2024-02-27](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10584-024-03699-1) **[P-peer-reviewed]**. It projects that **99% of properties in 1% AEP coastal inundation zones face at least partial insurance retreat within a decade** (with <10cm of SLR), and full retreat in 20-25 years.

Three things about it matter:
1. It is **New Zealand**, not the US.
2. It is **projected, not observed** — it models what insurers *would* do, not what any insurer *has* done.
3. **Its mechanism is not sea level rise itself.** The paper's causal chain is that SLR raises **event frequency** — a 1-in-100 storm tide becomes a 1-in-20 — and insurers withdraw when *expected annual loss* crosses a threshold. **That is loss experience.** SLR is the upstream cause; the trigger the insurer actually responds to is the frequency of the wet event.

**Conclusion:** the null result is not a gap in the evidence. It is the correct prediction of the model. **No insurer has ever been documented to withdraw on a sea-level projection, because no insurer needs to: a one-year contract can be repriced or non-renewed at the moment expected loss changes, so a carrier facing SLR simply waits.**

## 4. Synthesis — what actually triggers exit, ranked by evidence strength

| Rank | Mechanism | Evidence strength | Where it is the documented proximate cause |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **(a) Single large catastrophe year / loss-ratio shock** | **Strongest.** Direct, quantified, in primary company and auditor documents. | **Louisiana** — LLA names the 2020-21 hurricanes; 6 of 11 insolvencies traced directly to Ida. **California** — State Farm's $1.26-per-$1.00 nine-year loss experience and $5B cumulative underwriting loss; Milliman's −10.9% ten-year underwriting result. **Colorado** — Marshall Fire → FAIR Plan created. |
| **2** | **(c) Inability to get rate approved (regulatory friction)** | **Very strong in California specifically; near-zero elsewhere.** | **California only.** Named in the operative statements of State Farm ("outdated insurance regulations"), Allstate ("unable to quickly adjust prices due to state regulations"), and Chubb ("The state suppressed the industry's ability to charge a fair price"). **Proven by the reversal:** Farmers raised its cap 7,000→9,500/month in Dec 2024 citing the SIS regulations, with hazard unchanged. Florida, Louisiana and Texas do not have Prop 103, and no exit in those states is attributed to rate-approval friction. |
| **3** | **(d) Litigation environment** | **Very strong in Florida; near-zero elsewhere.** | **Florida only** — and it is decisive there. 9% of claims / 79% of suits (2020) → 4.85% / 41.29% (2025) after SB 2-A. Reversal with hurricane exposure unchanged, and after a $30-50B hurricane. |
| **4** | **(b) Reinsurance cost and availability** | **Strong as a transmission channel; weak as an independent cause.** | Universal in the *rhetoric* — named by State Farm, Allstate, Falls Lake ("could not obtain reinsurance"), and the LLA. But the Guy Carpenter US property-cat ROL index shows the 2022-24 hard market has reversed ~22% off the 2024 peak, and Florida's 2026 renewals came in 15-25% down — without producing re-entry in California. **Reinsurance amplifies; it rarely initiates.** Best-documented as a *sole* cause: Falls Lake, and the 6 Louisiana insolvencies. |
| **5** | **(e) Forward-looking model output / climate projections** | **Weakest. Essentially no direct evidence.** | **Zero documented cases as a proximate cause of exit.** Until 2024-12-13, California *forbade* using forward-looking cat models in ratemaking — so by construction, no California exit could have been driven by one. Where cat models do appear, it is on the *supply* side: carriers using coarser models "either charg[e] very high premiums or exit high-risk segments" [P-academic], i.e. the driver is **model uncertainty**, not a specific projected hazard level. |

**A qualifier the thesis should absorb.** Ranks 2 and 3 are both *institutional*, not physical. Between them they account for the two largest US market dislocations of the period. **The strongest single predictor of whether a state has an insurance crisis is not its hazard — it is whether its legal and rate-approval institutions let carriers price and defend claims.** California and Florida have roughly comparable catastrophe exposure and had opposite outcomes in 2024-2026 for purely institutional reasons.

**A second qualifier.** "Exit" is rarely a clean event. State Farm's March 2024 non-renewal covered ~2% of its California book. Insurer-initiated non-renewals run under 2% even in the highest-risk ZIPs. Carriers leave by **attrition and new-business suspension**, which is precisely why the *leading* indicators in §5.3 are the right thing to watch — the trailing indicator (a press release) arrives years after the balance sheet has already turned.

## 5. What it implies for Miami

### 5.1 Sea level rise is the slow variable; surge is the fast one

An insurance contract is **one year long**. A carrier's exposure to a hazard that changes by ~1-3% of expected loss per year is trivially manageable: reprice at renewal, or non-renew. The *only* thing that forces a carrier out is a loss that arrives faster than it can reprice — i.e. a single year in which realised loss exceeds several years of premium.

Concretely, at the tide gauge closest to downtown Miami (**NOAA 8723214, Virginia Key**), observed minor high-tide-flood days per year (this project's `noaa_high_tide_flooding_8723214.json`, NOAA data) **[P]**:

```
1995-2014:  0-3 days/yr (mostly 0)
2015: 3   2016: 3   2017: 3 (+1 moderate)   2018: 0   2019: 9
2020: 6   2021: 0   2022: 3   2023: 6   2024: 5   2025: 7
```

Nuisance flooding at Virginia Key has gone from ~0 days/year to ~5-7 days/year over a decade. That is a real, measurable, accelerating physical signal — and it is **exactly the kind of change an annually-repriced contract absorbs without anyone leaving.** Meanwhile, a single Category 4 landfall in Biscayne Bay would produce a multi-tens-of-billions insured loss in one day.

**So: sea level rise will not cause Miami's insurers to leave. It will cause Miami's insurers to reprice, slowly, until a surge event does the leaving for them — and it will make that surge event worse and more frequent.** The right way to represent SLR in the visualization's risk framing is as a **loss-amplifier on the surge distribution**, not as an independent exit driver.

The empirical support for this is Miami-Dade itself: over the very period in which SLR has been most visible, Citizens' Miami-Dade book fell **69%** as private carriers *took risk back*. If SLR projections drove insurer behaviour, that number would have the opposite sign.

### 5.2 Where flood differs: "exit" is consumer lapse, not carrier withdrawal

Because Florida residential flood is overwhelmingly written by the **NFIP — a federal programme with no shareholders, no rating agency, and no ability to withdraw from a market** — the entire California/Louisiana framework does not transfer. There is no carrier to leave. The failure mode is **on the demand side**:

1. **Consumer lapse under price pressure.** [Gourevitch, Snyder & Kousky, *Journal of Catastrophe Risk and Resilience*, 2025-12-16](https://journalofcrr.com/research/03-07-gourevitch-et-al/) **[P-peer-reviewed]** measure this directly: RR2.0 price increases reduced **new** flood policies by **11-39%** and **existing** policies by **5-13%**, with elasticities from −1.38 to −0.10 depending on the size of the increase. Losses were concentrated in the **lowest income quartile** (25-60% decline in new policies, vs 7-32% in the highest quartile).
2. **Programme-availability risk.** The NFIP's authorization **lapsed for 43 days, 2025-10-01 to 2025-11-13**, during the federal shutdown; no new or renewal policies could be issued. NAR estimated **~1,300 property sales/day and ~40,000 closings/month** affected. Reauthorized only to **2026-01-30** ([Insurance Journal](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/national/2025/11/13/847559.htm)) [S]. This has no private-market analogue and is arguably a larger near-term availability risk to Miami than any carrier decision.
3. **A slow, statutory price ramp that cannot be reversed by a good year.** Unlike wind, where 21 new Florida carriers entered and 44 filed rate decreases, flood premiums rise mechanically at up to 18%/yr until full-risk rates are met — with ~66% of policies still below full risk as of end-2022 and a median gap of ~90%.

**Private flood market share in Florida — what is and is not published.**
- Nationally, private flood share **more than doubled from just under 13% (2016) to 25% (2021)** and then **plateaued** — private insurers held **less than one-third of the overall US flood market in 2022 and 2023** ([AM Best special report, 2024-10-07, via Carrier Management](https://www.carriermanagement.com/news/2024/10/08/267359.htm)) [S reporting P report].
- **In Florida specifically, private insurers write less than 25% of flood premiums** (AM Best, same report). `[?]` **This is the most current Florida-specific private flood market share figure I could verify, and it is a premium share with a ">75% NFIP" framing rather than an exact number.**
- Historical Florida detail (now dated but primary): as of April 2018, Florida had **33,676 admitted primary residential flood policies + 5,983 excess**, plus ~24,400 E&S primary — **~58,000+ private residential flood policies, about 3% of Florida flood policies**, against ~1.7M NFIP ([Wharton Risk Center, Sept 2018](https://impact.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/Florida-Private-Flood-Issue-Brief.pdf)) **[P]**. Florida holds roughly **20% of all US private residential flood policies** [P-RFF].
- **`[!] FLOIR does not publish a private flood market share series.** Its [flood insurance page](https://floir.gov/property-casualty/flood-insurance) lists authorized carriers only and states that "Insurers writing private flood insurance in Florida voluntarily participate in data and loss ratio collection efforts." **To get a current, citable Florida private flood share you must request it: PrivateFlood@floir.com.**

**One Florida-specific structural change worth tracking.** SB 2-A phases in a **flood-coverage requirement for all Citizens policyholders**: SFHA new business from 2023-04-01, SFHA renewals from 2023-07-01, then by dwelling value — **$600k+ from 2024-01-01, $500k+ from 2025-01-01, $400k+ from 2026-01-01, and all remaining policies from 2027-01-01** ([Citizens](https://www.citizensfla.com/-/flood-insurance-coverage-rules-kick-in-for-citizens)) **[P]**. Only ~18% of Florida homeowners carried flood insurance in 2022. This mandate is a **confounder that will push flood take-up up for reasons unrelated to risk perception** — and it interacts with depopulation, since a policyholder taken out of Citizens leaves the mandate behind. Anyone modelling Miami flood take-up must control for it.

1. **USAA California withdrawal** — no documented new-business suspension or withdrawal found. Several roundups omit USAA entirely. **Do not assert one.**
2. **Whether State Farm General has resumed writing new California homeowners business as of Aug 2026** — not established. A narrow 2026 change lets some existing customers retain coverage after moving [S-SF Chronicle]; that is not a reopening.
3. **Whether NFIP Risk Rating 2.0 uses *projected* sea level rise or only current-condition flood frequency** — FEMA's published rating-variable list ("frequency of flood," "distance from a flooding source," "elevation") is silent on the temporal basis. congress.gov CRS product IN11777 was blocked by robots.txt. **Open question.**
4. **Guy Carpenter ROL index point values by year** — only percentage changes and cumulative comparisons are published free; the level series is behind Guy Carpenter's renewal-hub PDFs.
5. **Colorado FAIR Plan policy counts, and a Colorado post-Marshall non-renewal series** — not published by the FAIR Plan, the Colorado DOI, or DORA. Requires a direct request.
6. **A current, exact Florida private flood market share** — best available is AM Best's "<25% of flood premiums" (Oct 2024) [S reporting P]. FLOIR does not publish a series; request via PrivateFlood@floir.com.
7. **CDI Palisades/Eaton per-line claims breakdown** — the automated extraction of that PDF returned internally inconsistent line-of-business figures. Headline totals (38,835 residential claims; ~$22.0B paid) are reported; **re-read the PDF manually before quoting the split.**
